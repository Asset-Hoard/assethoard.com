26 November 2026 Indie life

Your indie game is a business the moment you charge for it

You get to invent worlds. You get to write stories and then hand the player the controls so they live them. You get to design mechanics that exist nowhere else, polish art that nobody has ever seen, and sweat over a sound effect for an hour because the impact has to land. You get to build the thing you wanted to play when you were fifteen and could not find.

Making indie games is one of the best creative jobs there is. Running an indie game business is a different job, and most devs start it by accident.

Then you put a price tag on it

For a lot of indies there comes a point where the game has to start paying for itself. Maybe the savings are running out. Maybe you want to make the next one without taking another contract. Maybe you just want the rent covered. Whatever the reason, you add a price, push the game onto a store, and something changes that catches most devs off guard.

It stops being only about you.

Up until that price tag, the game's job was to satisfy you. Your taste, your vision, your itch to make the thing. After the price tag, the game has a second job: convincing a stranger to part with money for it. The first job does not go away. The second one shows up uninvited and refuses to leave.

It is a product problem, not a game problem

If this feels unfair, take some comfort. It is not unique to games.

Anyone who has shipped a product, whether software, a book, a board game or a piece of hardware, has hit the same wall. The thing you made is one half of the work. Finding the people who want it, convincing them it is worth their money and earning enough trust that they click "buy" is the other half. Most first-time creators only plan for the first half.

Customers do not appear by themselves. Somebody has to go and find them, or, more honestly, give them a reason to find you. That work exists whether or not you have trained for it, and most of it is invisible to the people who play the game.

Your players are customers, and that is fine

There is a strain of indie thinking that treats "customer" as a dirty word. As if charging money for your game means you have sold out, or caring about reviews and retention is somehow uncreative.

It is neither. Treating the people who buy your game as customers means taking their experience seriously. It means reading the negative reviews carefully, not defensively. It means having a contact email that someone actually checks. It means patching the bug that only affects 2% of players, because to that 2%, it is 100% of their experience.

That said, you do not owe customers everything. You do not owe them a sequel, or every feature someone asks for in Discord. Running the product side means deciding, on purpose, what the game is and is not, and being able to say so out loud.

The muscle most devs have not built

You have probably seen this pattern in dev Discords and launch post-mortems. A dev spends two, three, sometimes five years building a game, staying motivated through the long middle. The whole time, the work is craft: code, art, sound, design, iteration. They get good at it. Deeply good.

Then the game is "done", and they pivot. Now they are going to "do marketing". They open a social account. They post a screenshot. Nothing happens. They try TikTok. Nothing happens. They email some press and get one reply out of forty. Building the game was hard too, but at least it responded to effort.

They are not bad at marketing. They are using a muscle they never trained, in the two weeks before launch, against people who have been training it for a decade. Of course it is hard. Nobody picks up a controller for the first time and wins a tournament.

Marketing and community are features

Think about how you plan a game. You make a list of features: mechanics, art style, sound design, levels, UI, the save system. Each one gets time and iteration, and each one gets debugged when it breaks.

Marketing and community engagement are features too. Not of the game. Of the product.

Trailer. Store page. Capsule art. Wishlist funnel. Devlogs. Discord. Press list. Streamer outreach. Demo. Festival submissions. Each one is a thing you build, iterate on and ship, and each one breaks in its own interesting ways. They do not live in the engine, but they are as real as your save system, and the game will not sell without them.

So put them on the same roadmap as everything else, with the same weight, alongside the game rather than after it.

The boring stuff is also your job

Under the marketing work sits a layer that is even less fun. Registering a business, whether as a sole trader, a company or an LLC. Tax registration. GST or VAT thresholds. Publisher contracts and what "exclusive" means in clause 4.3. Licences for every asset you shipped. Code signing, so Windows does not warn players off your installer. Refund policies. Invoices to collaborators. Bookkeeping.

None of it is fun. All of it is part of the job. You can outsource a lot of it to an accountant or a lawyer, and often you should. What you cannot do is pretend it is not there. The devs who get blindsided are the ones who tried to sort it all out in the same fortnight they were trying to ship.

Most of it, once set up, ticks along quietly in the background. Do it early and you forget about it. Do it late and it eats your launch.

What this looks like in practice

You do not need to become a marketer at the expense of being a developer. You need to spend a small, steady share of your time on the product side from the start, instead of none for years and all of it in the final fortnight.

A starting point we like, for a solo dev:

  • 70% making the game (about 28 hours of a 40-hour week)
  • 20% marketing and community (about 8 hours)
  • 10% operations, finances and admin (about 4 hours)

Early on, the 20% might just be a devlog and a Discord. It grows as launch gets closer. On a part-time schedule, scale the hours and keep the ratio. The exact numbers matter less than the habit: every week, some hours go to the parts of the job that are not building.

The indies who ship and earn a living from it are not necessarily better developers than the ones who do not. They are the ones who noticed they were running a product, and started treating it like one.
